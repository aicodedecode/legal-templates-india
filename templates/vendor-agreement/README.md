# Vendor Agreement

**Category:** Commercial & Enterprise · **Type:** agreement · **Jurisdiction:** India · **Status:** Draft

## Overview

A customer-leaning framework agreement for buying goods, services or both from a supplier, through purchase orders. It is drafted from the buyer's side and gives the buyer:

- non-binding forecasts, so there is no obligation to buy
- control over delivery, with time of the essence and liquidated damages for delay
- inspection and rejection rights
- early passing of title
- strong warranties and broad indemnities
- set-off rights
- termination for convenience

It also builds in the compliance terms Indian buyers increasingly need:

- MSMED Act payment discipline, with an MSE declaration form
- contract-labour compliance under the Labour Codes, with evidence of wage and PF/ESI payments
- GST input tax credit protection
- anti-bribery and sanctions undertakings
- audit rights
- DPDP processor terms
- optional ESG and value-chain reporting

## When to use it

- You regularly buy materials, packaging, components, equipment, IT hardware, facility management, housekeeping, security, logistics, marketing or similar goods or services from the same supplier.
- You want a single signed framework, with each order placed by purchase order rather than renegotiated.
- Supplier personnel will work at your premises, so you need contract-labour protections.

## When not to use it

- **Software subscriptions.** Use the saas-subscription-agreement, negotiated from the customer side.
- **Complex IT development or managed services.** Use the master-services-agreement with a statement-of-work. Its IP and acceptance mechanics fit better.
- **Appointing a distributor or reseller of your products.** Use the distribution-agreement.
- **Purchases from individual consultants.** Use the consultancy-agreement.
- **Real-estate, construction or EPC works.** These need specialised contracts dealing with site risk, performance guarantees, and statutory approvals and registration.

## Key provisions

- **Purchase orders and precedence (cl. 2, Sch. 3).** Purchase orders are deemed accepted if not rejected within a set time. The buyer can change or cancel an order, but pays for custom work in progress. The supplier's terms are excluded.
- **Goods (cl. 3).** The supplier must meet the specifications and the implied quality conditions, comply with BIS and legal metrology labelling, provide e-way bills, and meet delivery dates (time is of the essence). Liquidated damages for delay are capped and framed as a genuine pre-estimate under s.74. The buyer can buy substitutes at the supplier's cost.
- **Inspection and rejection (cl. 5).** The buyer may inspect before dispatch, has an inspection window after delivery, has a range of rejection remedies, and keeps its rights for latent defects. The inspection window is aligned with the MSMED Act deemed-acceptance rule.
- **Title and risk (cl. 6).** These depart from the Sale of Goods Act defaults. Risk passes on unloading; title passes on delivery or payment. The supplier warrants that the goods are free of encumbrances.
- **Payment (cl. 7).**
  - GST is charged in addition to the price, and the supplier indemnifies the buyer against loss of input tax credit.
  - TDS and TCS are handled generically.
  - Bank-detail changes must be verified by call-back, as a fraud control.
  - The MSE override applies the 45-day limit and s.16 interest. The drafting note explains the tax disallowance for late payment to MSEs, MSME Form 1 and the MSEFC route.
- **Labour compliance (cl. 9).** The clause covers licences, wages, PF/ESI, records and POSH, with monthly evidence of payment. The buyer can pay workers directly and recover the cost. The drafting note covers principal employer liability under the Labour Codes.
- **Compliance and ESG (cl. 10, Sch. 4).** Anti-bribery, the supplier code of conduct, sanctions and export control, import and FEMA documentation, and optional ESG and BRSR data obligations.
- **Audit (cl. 11).** Audit rights extend to the buyer's regulators. The supplier pays the audit cost and refunds overcharges where the audit finds a problem.
- **IP (cl. 12).** Bespoke materials are assigned in compliance with s.19 (full term, worldwide, no lapse), with a moral-rights waiver and a licence of the supplier's background IP.
- **Data (cl. 14).** DPDP processor terms, breach notice within a set number of hours, and CERT-In compliance.
- **Indemnity and liability (cl. 15–16).** Broad indemnities covering IP, product liability under the CPA 2019, recalls, labour, compliance, data and GST. The clause sets out how claims are conducted and when the buyer can step back into the defence. Caps are asymmetric. The supplier has a separate super-cap for data, confidentiality and IP, and specified losses are treated as direct.
- **Termination and exit (cl. 18–19).** The buyer can terminate for convenience or for cause, including on a change of control or insolvency. The supplier can terminate only for prolonged non-payment. Exit supply and transition last up to 90 days, with spares available for 5 years.
- **Disputes (cl. 22–23).** Arbitration with the seat and venue stated separately and no unilateral appointment. The MSEFC route is preserved for MSE suppliers, and s.12A mediation applies to court suits.

## India-specific notes

- **Stamp duty.** The agreement is chargeable under the Stamp Act of the State where it is signed. Some States levy ad valorem duty on supply or works contracts, and purchase orders may attract duty. Stamp at or before signing through e-stamping. Unstamped instruments are inadmissible until duty and penalty are paid, but are not void (*In re Interplay*, 2023).
- **MSMED Act.** The Act's payment provisions override the contract (s.24). The MSEFC can take jurisdiction despite the arbitration clause (*Mahakali Foods*, 2022). Paying MSEs late has tax consequences under applicable tax laws, and outstanding dues may have to be reported in MSME Form 1. Collect a Udyam declaration (Sch. 5) at onboarding, and refresh it every year.
- **Labour Codes.** If a contractor defaults, the principal employer may be liable for its workers' wages and social security. Monthly evidence of payment, and limiting direct control over contractor staff, reduce both that risk and the risk of being found to have a "sham contract".
- **Consumer Protection Act, 2019.** If the goods reach consumers, product liability under Chapter VI and recalls ordered by the CCPA can fall on the buyer as product seller or manufacturer. Back-to-back indemnities and recall-cost recovery are essential.
- **Liquidated damages.** Courts award reasonable compensation up to the agreed cap (s.74). Record why delay causes loss.
- **E-signature and evidence.** Electronic execution is valid. Keep audit trails for agreements, purchase orders and invoices, because a certificate under s.63 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 may be needed to prove them.
- **Non-exclusivity.** The agreement places no restraint on either party, which avoids issues under s.27 of the Contract Act and the Competition Act.

## Customisation checklist

- [ ] Verify the supplier's legal name, GSTIN, PAN and Udyam status, and collect Schedule 5.
- [ ] Complete Schedules 1 and 2: items, specifications, prices, HSN/SAC codes and the TDS/TCS category.
- [ ] Set the purchase order acceptance time, the inspection window (15 days or less if the supplier is an MSE) and the warranty period.
- [ ] Set the payment days (45 days or less for MSE suppliers).
- [ ] Set the delay liquidated damages rate and cap.
- [ ] Set the supplier cap, the super-cap multiple and the insurance limits.
- [ ] Decide whether to keep the optional ESG clause (cl. 10.6).
- [ ] Confirm the arbitration seat, venue and appointment route.
- [ ] Decide who pays stamp duty, and whether purchase orders need stamping.
- [ ] Execute a DPA if the supplier will handle personal data.
